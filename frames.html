Frame theory · Linear algebra · 2014–2017

Splitting a frame, and keeping it balanced.

Before neurons, my research was pure mathematics. A frame is a redundant set of vectors that still spans the space. In 2015 Marcus, Spielman and Srivastava proved that any such set can be split into pieces that are each spread out evenly, which solved the 50-year-old Kadison–Singer problem. But their proof doesn't say how to find the split. My master's thesis and our paper built the first efficient algorithm for a well-known family of frames.

01 · What a frame is

More vectors than you need, on purpose.

A basis of the plane has exactly two vectors. A frame can have many, pointing in many directions. The redundancy is the point: if a signal is sent as its inner products with the frame vectors and some are lost or noisy, the rest still recover it. That is why frames run through signal processing, coding and sensor networks.

A harmonic frame in the plane

The vectors f_j=\sqrt{2/m}\,(\cos\frac{2\pi j}{m},\sin\frac{2\pi j}{m}) are spaced evenly around the circle. With m=3 it is the Mercedes-Benz frame.

1.000

Wherever you point x, the bars add up to exactly \lVert x\rVert^2=1: the frame is Parseval, as balanced as an orthonormal basis.

Frames and frame bounds

Vectors \{f_j\}_{j=1}^n in \mathbb C^m form a frame if there are constants 0<A\le B with

A\lVert x\rVert^2\le\sum_{j=1}^n|\langle x,f_j\rangle|^2\le B\lVert x\rVert^2\quad\text{for every }x.

The frame is tight if A=B and Parseval if A=B=1, which is the same as its frame operator being the identity:

\sum_{j=1}^n f_j f_j^{*}=I_m.

Why the plane example is Parseval

For x=(\cos\phi,\sin\phi),

\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\frac{2}{m}\cos^2\!\Big(\phi-\frac{2\pi j}{m}\Big)=\frac1m\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\Big(1+\cos\big(2\phi-\tfrac{4\pi j}{m}\big)\Big)=1,

since the cosines of m evenly spaced angles sum to zero (for m\ge3).

02 · The question

Can every balanced frame be split into balanced pieces?

The Kadison–Singer problem (1959) asked about extending states of operator algebras. Weaver showed it is equivalent to a question about splitting vectors, and in 2015 Marcus, Spielman and Srivastava answered it. Their proof shows that a good split exists, but gives no way to find one. Finding one efficiently was left open at the end of their paper.

The MSS theorem (2015)

If v_1,\dots,v_n\in\mathbb C^m satisfy \sum_j v_jv_j^*=I and \lVert v_j\rVert^2\le\delta, then for every r there is a partition S_1,\dots,S_r with

\Big\lVert\sum_{j\in S_k}v_jv_j^*\Big\rVert\le\Big(\frac1{\sqrt r}+\sqrt\delta\Big)^2\quad\text{for all }k.

Each piece's outer Gramian F_S=\sum_{j\in S}f_jf_j^* stays bounded: no piece hoards too much of the space.

Not every split works

The theorem only says a good partition exists. In the 32-vector frame of the paper, the 16-vector subset

S=\{2,5,7,8,9,10,11,13,14,16,17,19,21,25,27,31\}

with 16 singletons (r=17) has \lVert F_S\rVert=0.998, above the MSS bound 0.902. You can try it below.

The Rado–Horn theorem

The classical side of the story: a frame splits into K linearly independent sets exactly when every subset J has

\frac{|J|}{\dim\operatorname{span}(F_J)}\le K.

It says nothing about the norms of the pieces. Our partitions are both: linearly independent and bounded.

03 · The thesis

Splitting the circle in two.

For the harmonic frame of m vectors, the thesis computes the norm of any run of n consecutive vectors exactly, and asks how long the run can be before the split into two pieces (r=2, \delta=2/m) breaks the MSS bound.

An exact formula

The outer Gramian of the run S_1=\{f_0,\dots,f_{n-1}\} is a 2×2 matrix whose largest eigenvalue sums a geometric series of e^{4\pi ij/m}:

\begin{gathered}\lVert F_{S_1}\rVert=\frac nm+\frac{\sin(2\pi n/m)}{m\,\sin(2\pi/m)},\\\lVert F_{S_2}\rVert=\frac{m-n}m+\frac{\sin(2\pi n/m)}{m\,\sin(2\pi/m)}.\end{gathered}

How long can the run be?

n_{\max}=\lfloor x\rfloor, where x solves the non-elementary equation

\frac xm+\frac{\sin(2\pi x/m)}{m\sin(2\pi/m)}=\Big(\frac1{\sqrt2}+\sqrt{\frac2m}\Big)^2,

solved numerically (the thesis used Mathematica, this page uses bisection). For m=100, n_{\max}=84; as m\to\infty, n_{\max}/m\to\tfrac12. Even and almost-even splits always pass.

04 · The paper

Equiangular frames, and a partition you can compute.

Equiangular tight frames (ETFs) are the most spread-out frames there are: every pair of vectors meets at the same angle, as small as possible. We built them from a recursive skew-symmetric conference matrix, and showed that cutting its Gram matrix into blocks along the diagonal gives partitions that beat the MSS bound.

Partition an ETF

Largest \lVert F_{S_h}\rVert
Our bound \tfrac12+\tfrac1{\sqrt{2r}} (diagonal partitions)
MSS bound (1/\sqrt r+1/\sqrt2)^2

    The Welch bound

    For n unit vectors in \mathbb C^m, the largest correlation can't be smaller than

    \max_{j\ne l}|\langle f_j,f_l\rangle|\ \ge\ \sqrt{\frac{n-m}{m(n-1)}},

    with equality exactly for equiangular tight frames (Strohmer and Heath): the best possible packings of lines.

    A recursive conference matrix

    Start from C(1)=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix} and double:

    \begin{gathered}C(k)=\begin{pmatrix}C(k-1)&C(k-1)-I\\C(k-1)+I&-C(k-1)\end{pmatrix},\\C C^{\mathsf T}=(n-1)I.\end{gathered}

    It is skew-symmetric with spectrum \pm i\sqrt{n-1}, each of multiplicity n/2.

    From the matrix to the frame

    With \alpha=1/\sqrt{n-1}, the Hermitian matrix R=I+i\alpha C has eigenvalues

    \sigma(R)=\{1\pm\alpha\sqrt{n-1}\}=\{0,\,2\},

    each n/2 times. Its eigenvectors for 2 give n=2m vectors in \mathbb C^m: a Parseval ETF with \lVert f_j\rVert^2=\delta=\tfrac12, whose Gram matrix is \tfrac12R.

    The main theorem

    A subset's norm is half the norm of its block of R. For a diagonal block of size 2^{k-d} that block is again R(k-d), so

    \lVert F_{S}\rVert=\frac12+\frac12\sqrt{\frac{2^{k-d}-1}{2^k-1}}\ \le\ \frac12+\frac1{\sqrt{2r}}\ \le\ \Big(\frac1{\sqrt r}+\frac1{\sqrt2}\Big)^2.

    Every diagonal partition with subsets of at most 2^{k-\lfloor\log_2r\rfloor} vectors is an MSS partition, of linearly independent pieces, with a sharper bound.

    Algorithm 1: linear time

    Let d=\lfloor\log_2 r\rfloor. Cut the first 2(r-2^d) blocks of size 2^{k-d-1}, then the remaining 2^{d+1}-r of size 2^{k-d}:

    2(r-2^d)\,2^{k-d-1}+(2^{d+1}-r)\,2^{k-d}=2^k.

    Each block is a run of consecutive indices, so the partition is built in one pass.

    Fusion frames

    The pieces' spans form a fusion frame, the structure behind distributed sensor networks, with bounds

    \begin{gathered}A=\frac1{\min_h A_h},\qquad B=\frac1{\max_h B_h},\\A_h,B_h=\frac12\mp\frac12\sqrt{\frac{2^{k-d_h}-1}{2^k-1}}.\end{gathered}

    05 · Small pieces always work

    Any partition into pairs or triples is safe.

    For any ETF, not only ours, the outer Gramian norm of a pair or a triple can be computed exactly, and it always beats the MSS bound. The chart compares all the bounds for \delta=\tfrac12, as in the paper's Fig. 2.

    Pairs (Lemma 7.1, Theorem 7.2)

    \lVert F_{\{j,l\}}\rVert=\frac mn\Big(1+\sqrt{\frac{n-m}{m(n-1)}}\Big)\ \le\ \delta+\sqrt{\frac\delta r},\qquad\delta=\frac mn.

    Triples (Lemma 7.3, Theorem 7.4)

    \begin{gathered}\lVert F_{S}\rVert=\frac mn\Big(1+\sqrt{\frac{\ell\,(n-m)}{m(n-1)}}\Big)\ \le\ \delta+\sqrt{\frac{\ell\delta}r},\\\ell=\begin{cases}1\text{ or }2&\text{in }\mathbb R\\3&\text{in }\mathbb C\end{cases}\end{gathered}

    From four vectors on, the norms are no longer uniform, and whether every partition passes is still open.

    06 · What's still open

    Where it could go next.

    Consecutive is safe?

    The thesis conjectures that, for these Grassmannian frames, partitions into runs of consecutive vectors are always valid, and that non-consecutive ones whose largest piece has at least half the vectors are not.

    Pieces of four or more

    Exact norms for subsets of size four and up, and with them a proof that every such partition of an ETF meets the MSS bound, or a counterexample.

    Other frames

    Efficient MSS partitions for Gabor frames, and fast norm computations for very large Grassmannian frames.
