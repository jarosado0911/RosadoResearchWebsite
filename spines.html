Research · Dendritic spines

Calcium in dendritic spines.

Dendritic spines are the tiny protrusions where most excitatory synapses in the brain sit. Many of the larger ones contain a spine apparatus, a stack of ER that reaches from the dendrite into the spine. My ultrastructural simulations of these reconstructed spines showed that the spine apparatus can act as an all-or-nothing switch for calcium signals between the spine head and the dendrite.

Simulation

One spine sets off a calcium wave.

A simulation of calcium on a spine with a spine apparatus, attached to a long dendrite with ER inside it. The shapes are shown cut open down the middle. Top: a close-up of the spine. Bottom: the whole dendrite, with the close-up area boxed.

Calcium in the ER and spine apparatus 0 µM250 µM

  1. At rest

    The cytosol sits at about 50 nM of calcium (blue), while the ER and the spine apparatus are full, at about 250 µM (magenta).

  2. A synapse fires

    Calcium flows in at the synapse on the spine head and spreads down the thin neck. The spine apparatus in the head releases its calcium too.

  3. The dendrite answers

    Calcium reaching the dendrite at the base of the spine opens ryanodine receptors on the dendritic ER, which releases more calcium (calcium-induced calcium release).

  4. A wave in both directions

    Each stretch of ER that releases raises the calcium enough to trigger the next stretch, so two fronts travel away from the spine, and the ER empties behind them.

  5. Recovery

    The fronts reach both ends. Pumps and buffers bring the cytosol back toward rest, while the ER stays empty: refilling it takes longer than this simulation shows.
