Multi-scale modeling · TMS · Brain Stimulation 2021

One pulse, three scales.

Transcranial magnetic stimulation is widely used, but how a neuron responds to it is still poorly understood. NeMo-TMS, an open-source toolbox from a team led by Alexander Opitz, follows one pulse from the electric field in the head, to the voltage of a single realistic neuron, to the calcium inside it. I wrote the calcium part. The cell behind this text is replaying a spike and the passive calcium that follows it.

The chain

Each tool hands its answer to the next.

No single simulator covers centimeters of head and micrometers of cell, so the toolbox connects four steps.

01

SimNIBS · finite elements

The field in the head

A coil's changing current induces an electric field, computed across a model of the head or a dish of tissue. At these frequencies its pattern in space and its shape in time can be treated separately.

02

MATLAB · the coupling

From a field to a neuron

The cell is placed in the model, by default upright against the grey matter surface. The field is read at each segment and summed along the tree into quasipotentials, which NEURON can use:

\psi_i=\psi_{\text{parent}}-\tfrac12\big(E_i+E_{\text{parent}}\big)\cdot s_i
03

NEURON · 5 or 25 µs steps

The membrane answers

Quasipotentials times the pulse's shape become the extracellular potential of every segment of a realistic model cell. NEURON saves the membrane voltage everywhere, at every step.

04

UG4 · my part

Calcium follows

Those voltages open calcium channels. Calcium flows in, diffuses, is buffered and is pumped out, solved as diffusion-reaction equations on the 1D tree of the cell.

The model ↓

One run

Passive calcium: it glows where the spike goes.

This run demonstrates passive calcium behavior only. Voltage opens channels, calcium flows in, diffuses, is buffered and is pumped out. The ER is off, so nothing releases more calcium and nothing can carry it onward as a wave. Drag across a chart to move the neuron in the background to that moment.

  1. 0.38 ms

    The first spike appears far out on the axon, about 1,090 µm from the soma.

  2. 1.15 ms

    It reaches the soma, peaking near +41 mV.

  3. 2.45 ms

    Calcium peaks. At the soma, about 0.93 µM, up from 0.05 µM at rest. Across 97% of the axon it at least doubles, to a median of about 0.13 µM, then fades close to rest by about 5 ms.

  4. Dendrites

    Far dendrites stay dark. The spike fades as it enters them, to about −37 mV by 60–150 µm from the soma, too little to open many channels.

  5. A real wave?

    Waves need the ER to release calcium. See one driven by a single synapse in the CalcSim model.

How this run differs from the toolbox: its script keeps the axon's calcium at rest, while here the axon has the same channels, pumps, buffer and diffusion as the rest of the cell, including stretches that would be myelinated, so the calcium can be seen across the whole cell. The saved voltage lasts 2.9 ms; after that each point relaxes to rest with a 5 ms time constant, an approximation of mine so the glow can fade. The sample did not record its stimulus settings or step; its 120 frames are taken as 25 µs apart, the toolbox's default, which gives a spike of a realistic length.

Aim the field

Direction decides where a neuron starts.

In the paper's third example, the field along the cell's axis fired it at 275 V/m, from the long axon's terminal. Turned 45°, it took 280 V/m and the spike started at a different terminal, the one better aligned with the field. Try it on three of the toolbox's cells.

The calcium model

What the voltage sets in motion.

A cut along a dendrite, as my script sets it up. Calcium modeling matters here because NEURON models usually lack it or simplify it, leaving out details such as its diffusion.

In the toolbox's script these mechanisms are on the soma and dendrites; this page's run adds them to the axon. The solver halves its time step when a Newton solve fails and doubles it again after a run of successes, so it can take small steps through a spike. Voltage passes one way, from NEURON to the calcium model, so its step can differ from NEURON's.

From the paper

What the examples showed.

A tissue culture in a dish

A spike that starts far from the soma

A single pulse depolarized an axon terminal enough to fire. Through the myelinated axon, the spike reached the soma around 1 ms later, and dendrites followed by diffusion.

10 Hz against theta burst

Two routes to a calcium build-up

Both protocols built up somatic calcium over time, in different patterns. Initial tissue-culture experiments showed the 10 Hz rise too; the authors call for fuller validation.

A uniform field, two angles

Orientation moves the starting point

275 V/m along the somatodendritic axis, 280 V/m at 45°, a different starting terminal, and a shift of about 0.1 ms that also delayed the calcium.

A human neuron in a head model

Not every pulse fires

In a 10 Hz protocol over the motor cortex, the neuron's slow recovery meant it did not fire with every pulse. Synaptic input timed to the pulses lowered its threshold.
