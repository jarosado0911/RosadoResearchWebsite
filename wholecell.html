Research · Dendritic spines · The full simulation

One synapse, a whole neuron.

My spine models, scaled up to a whole reconstructed cell: its real plasma membrane, an ER tube inside every dendrite, 1.47 million tetrahedra. One synapse lets calcium in, and the ER carries it on as a wave, into the soma and out to the tips of the dendrites. All but one. The background is that simulation, replayed from its own output. 01 · The wave

Follow the calcium.

Scroll through the run. The background flies to each part of the cell and plays that stretch of time; the player at the bottom right pauses it or scrubs through all 100 ms.

  1. 01

    The cell

    A rat layer-5 pyramidal neuron, 0-2a from NeuroMorpho.org: its membrane meshed with AnaMorph, the soma shrunk to a 4.8 µm radius, an ER tube at 0.4 times the radius inside every dendrite and an ER ball of 3.4 µm inside the soma. Six dendrites leave the soma. d5, the thick apical dendrite, runs 480 µm to the right.

    The membrane glows with the free calcium in the cytosol just inside it. The ER is mint green while it is full, at 250 µM, and darkens as it empties.

  2. 02

    A synapse opens

    syn1 sits on dendrite d4, 30 µm from the soma center. For 10 ms it lets calcium in, and IP3 for 200 ms. Calcium there reaches 67 µM at 8 ms. Under it, ryanodine receptors on the ER open and release more calcium: calcium-induced calcium release. Their open probability is a steep switch,

    P_R(c)=\frac{1+K_Bc^3}{1+K_C+(K_Ac^4)^{-1}+K_Bc^3}

    almost shut at rest (3×10⁻⁴ at 50 nM), half open at 1.5 µM. Two fronts leave syn1, toward the soma and toward the tip, at 2.5–3 µm per ms.

  3. 03

    The wave reaches the soma

    The front arrives at 7.5–8 ms. The soma's membrane flashes to about 2 µM at 10 ms, then settles at 0.1–0.4 µM while the front passes: the soma is large, and its own release is slow. It keeps filling for the rest of the run, to 2.1 µM at 100 ms.

  4. 04

    Inside the soma

    The membranes peel back to the same cut as in the video: through the soma center along the incoming dendrite, bent by at most 0.6 µm to follow it down to syn1. On the cut is the calcium inside the volume, in the cytosol and in the ER lumen.

    The soma ER empties from the outside in: 250 µM at rest, 151 at 20 ms, 57 at 50 ms, 24 at 100 ms. The cytosol is brightest right next to the ER membrane, where the release happens.

  5. 05

    Out along the thin dendrites

    From the soma the wave enters the thin dendrites d0–d3 at 10–12 ms and runs at a steady 2.7–2.8 µm per ms, 5–8 µM at the front. Behind it the ER is down to 30–45 µM. The front reaches the tip of d3 at 50 ms and the end of d1, 181 µm out, at 78 ms.

  6. 06

    The thick dendrite gives up

    d5 is five times wider than the thin dendrites, 1.5–1.8 µm in radius. What enters it reaches only 0.5–0.7 µM at the membrane, slows to 1.6 µm per ms, and stops 25–28 µm from the soma center. Next to its ER the peak falls from 6.4 to 1.9 µM over those 20 µm.

    This is an abortive wave, as in Breit and Queisser's parameter study (2018). Why it happens ↓

  7. 07

    100 ms later

    The thin dendrites sit at a raised 0.2–0.3 µM with their ER mostly emptied; across the cell the ER is down from 250 to 103 µM on average. Beyond its first 40 µm, d5 never noticed. Refilling the ER takes far longer than this run.

04 · Why it stops

A thick dendrite dilutes its store.

The ER is 0.4 times the radius everywhere, and every channel and pump has the same density per µm² of membrane. But membrane grows with the radius and volume with its square, so per µm³ of cytosol the thick dendrite has five times less ER membrane, and five times fewer ryanodine receptors, than a thin one.

Membrane per volume

\alpha_e=\frac{A_{\mathrm{ER}}}{V_{\mathrm{cyt}}}=\frac{2\rho}{R\,(1-\rho^2)},\qquad \alpha_p=\frac{A_{\mathrm{PM}}}{V_{\mathrm{cyt}}}=\frac{\alpha_e}{\rho}

For a tube of radius R with its ER at \rho R, \rho=0.4. Every membrane flux per volume of cytosol falls as 1/R: release, uptake and extrusion alike. Calcium binding to calbindin and diffusion happen in the volume and do not.

Thick is thin with faster buffer

t=\tfrac{R}{R_0}\,\tau,\quad x=\sqrt{\tfrac{R}{R_0}}\;\xi\ \Longrightarrow\ k_{\mathrm{on}},\,k_{\mathrm{off}}\ \to\ \tfrac{R}{R_0}\,k_{\mathrm{on}},\,\tfrac{R}{R_0}\,k_{\mathrm{off}}

Rescaling time and space turns the cable equations of a dendrite of radius R into those of one of radius R_0, except that calbindin binds R/R_0 times faster. d5 behaves like a thin dendrite whose buffer is 5.7 times faster, and that dendrite has no wave.

The race: release against binding

\Lambda(c)=\frac{\alpha_e\,j_{\mathrm{ER}}(c,e_0,p_0)+\alpha_p\,j_{\mathrm{PM}}(c)}{k_{\mathrm{on}}\,b_0\,(c-c_0)}

Where \Lambda>1 the release raises free calcium faster than calbindin (160 µM of sites, a buffer capacity of about 200) can bind it, the ryanodine receptors see the excess, and the release feeds itself. Its maximum, near 2 µM where the receptor opens, is 3.1 in the thin dendrites, 2.3 in d4, 1.4 in d2's wide stretch (which carried a weakened wave) and 0.55 in d5, where the release never wins.

What could carry it further

Tested first in a reduced 1D cable model with the same transporters, which confirms the picture: with this calbindin, waves exist up to a radius of 0.4 µm and fail from 0.6 µm, whatever the stimulus.

Most direct

Receptors that scale with radius

Give d5's ER more ryanodine receptors per µm², in proportion to its radius, and rebalance SERCA so the resting state holds. In the cable model it is the only single change that also starts a wave from the soma's own drive.

Anatomical

A thicker ER

An ER at 0.6–0.7 times the radius in the thick dendrite puts more store membrane against less cytosol, and its large store keeps releasing behind the front.

Check first

A finer mesh in d5

d5's cytosol ring between ER and membrane is only about one tetrahedron wide, so the thin layer next to the ER, where the release briefly does win, is under-resolved. The 3D result may be pessimistic.

05 · The model

Four species, two compartments, every membrane.

The whole-cell version of the spine model from the PLOS Computational Biology paper, in UG4 with the neuro_collection plugin: free calcium c, the buffer calbindin b and IP3 p in the cytosol, calcium e in the ER lumen. Every channel and pump sits on the real 3D membranes.

In the volumes

\begin{aligned}\partial_t c&=\nabla\!\cdot(D_c\nabla c)-k_{\mathrm{on}}cb+k_{\mathrm{off}}(B_{\mathrm{tot}}-b)\\ \partial_t b&=\nabla\!\cdot(D_b\nabla b)-k_{\mathrm{on}}cb+k_{\mathrm{off}}(B_{\mathrm{tot}}-b)\\ \partial_t p&=\nabla\!\cdot(D_p\nabla p)-k_p(p-p_0)\\ \partial_t e&=\nabla\!\cdot(D_e\nabla e)\end{aligned}

Calbindin holds 160 µM of binding sites; at rest it buffers 199 of every 200 calcium ions, within about 0.25 ms.

On the membranes

\begin{gathered}D_c\,\partial_nc=j_{\mathrm{ER}}=-D_e\,\partial_{n_e}e\quad\text{on the ER},\\ j_{\mathrm{ER}}=\rho_RJ_R+\rho_IJ_I+\kappa_e(e-c)-\rho_SJ_S,\\ D_c\,\partial_nc=\kappa_p(c_o-c)-\rho_PJ_P-\rho_NJ_N\ (+j_{\mathrm{syn}})\end{gathered}

Ryanodine and IP3 receptors release, SERCA pumps back into the ER; PMCA and NCX pump out of the cell. SERCA's density is set so that every flux balances at rest.

A positivity guard (my change to neuro_collection)

g_\varepsilon(x)=\frac{x+\sqrt{x^2+\varepsilon^2}}{2}=\frac{\varepsilon^2/4}{\sqrt{x^2+\varepsilon^2}-x},\qquad \varepsilon=10^{-12}\ \mathrm{mM}

On this mesh 22.8% of the edges give the diffusion matrix a positive off-diagonal entry, so the finite volume scheme is not monotone and steep fronts can undershoot below zero. The transporter formulas then misbehave: SERCA divides by the ER's calcium, the receptors raise negative numbers to powers. Their concentration inputs now pass through a smooth positive part, written in its second, cancellation-free form for negative x. Above a picomolar it changes nothing (relative change below \varepsilon^2/4x^2), and calcium stays conserved, since the same guarded flux leaves one compartment and enters the other. It let the run past the step where it had aborted at 11 ms; the dip itself, down to −0.27 µM next to the soma, needs a better mesh.
